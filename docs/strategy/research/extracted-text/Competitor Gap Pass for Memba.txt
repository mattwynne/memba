Competitor Gap Pass for Memba
Research date: May 2026. The gap pass changes the picture materially. The strongest newly-surfaced
threats are not the legacy AMS names; they are a combination of free or donor-funded platforms that
crush the price floor, Commonwealth club-native systems that fit volunteer sports/recreation more
closely than US nonprofit tools do, and lighter-weight community/event products that are already
winning informal outdoor and activity groups. In plain terms: Memba should assume that a prospect can
already choose $0, very cheap club-native software, or a free social/event stack plus Stripe, and should
only count on winning if it delivers a meaningfully better domain model for households, renewals, privacy,
waivers, volunteer handoff, and exportability. 1


My highest-confidence conclusions are these. Pricing differentiation is substantially weakened by Zeffy,
HelloAsso, Spond Club, Givebutter’s free core CRM, and very low-cost club-native tools like membermojo
and Clubforce. Household/family differentiation survives because most competitors still treat “family” as
either a billing convenience, a lead-member-plus-linked-records workaround, or a shared account with
blunt permissions rather than a true household model. Volunteer-admin handoff remains under-served
almost everywhere. Outdoor/activity-specific workflows—leader roles, member-only visibility, waitlists,
waivers, emergency contact handling, and lightweight trip approvals in one system—are still poorly
integrated across the field, even where pieces exist. Confidence: high on price compression; medium-high
on household and workflow gaps; medium on some undocumented absences in smaller vendors’ public
docs. 2


Executive assessment
For Memba’s Canadian outdoor-club beachhead, the most credible immediate substitutes are TidyHQ,
Amilia, Heylo, Spond, membermojo, and—where the club looks more like a governed sport body than a
volunteer association—SportLoMo and Clubforce. Zeffy is the single biggest pricing threat, but not the
single biggest replacement threat: it is extremely strong as a no-fee payment/form/membership-on-ramp,
yet much weaker as a club operating system. HelloAsso matters strategically because it proves a voluntary-
contribution model can work at very large scale, but it is operationally France-only. Givebutter and
Donorbox are credible substitutes for nonprofits whose “membership” is really recurring giving plus events
and email, but they are not clean replacements for householded member organizations that need club-
native privacy and activity workflows. 3


The revised product thesis should therefore be: do not sell “fair pricing” as the primary wedge. Sell “the
club model others don’t actually model”—especially household/family complexity, renewal edge cases,
volunteer turnover, and lightweight activity/waiver/privacy workflows that feel designed for real volunteer
organizations rather than for generic fundraising, generic recreation, or chat-first groups. 4

Zeffy deep dive

What is real about Zeffy’s “free forever” claim

Zeffy’s “free forever” claim is not marketing sleight-of-hand in the narrow sense. In the official company
materials reviewed here, Zeffy says it charges no transaction fees, no platform fees, no monthly fees,
and no hidden costs, and that its economics are funded by voluntary donor contributions rather than
nonprofit charges. In another official explanation, Zeffy says that two out of three donors leave a
contribution to support Zeffy. That makes the model real in the sense that the nonprofit can genuinely see a
$0 software bill and $0 payment-fee deduction from Zeffy itself. 6


The sustainability signal is meaningful, too. Zeffy’s official materials claim 100,000+ nonprofits use the
platform. That is enough adoption to treat the model as proven, not theoretical. Still, I did not find a public
audited breakdown of contribution conversion, average contribution percentage, gross margin, or long-
term cohort economics in the sources reviewed, so any claim beyond “the model exists and appears to work
at scale” would be overstated. Confidence: high on the pricing mechanics; medium on long-run durability
because the economics disclosed publicly are still top-line, not audited operating detail. 6


What nonprofits actually pay in practice

For the nonprofit organization itself, the headline price can genuinely be zero. The economic “catch” is
shifted to the payer experience: the donor or member is asked to support Zeffy voluntarily at checkout.
Zeffy explicitly says the contribution is voluntary, and that user generosity is what keeps the platform free.
In practice, this means a club using Zeffy can tell itself that software and processing are free, while some
portion of supporters absorb extra spend through the optional contribution prompt. That is strategically
important for Memba because it means “no Memba surcharge on payments” is not enough by itself; Zeffy
can counter with “we don’t surcharge the organization at all.” 6


The right competitive reading is therefore not “Zeffy is fake-free”; it is “Zeffy is organizationally free but
checkout-mediated.” That distinction matters. Some volunteer clubs will love it. Others—especially
member-funded clubs with recurring community relationships—may dislike asking members to subsidize
the software provider at every payment interaction. That emotional and governance discomfort is likely the
surviving angle for Memba, but it is a secondary angle, not a primary wedge. 6


Where Zeffy still falls short for Memba’s buyer

The audited public evidence in this pass does not show Zeffy matching the club-operating requirements
that matter most to Memba’s beachhead: first-class households, per-person member access in a family
context, club directory privacy, volunteer succession controls, and outdoor-club activity workflows like trip
leaders, expressions of interest, approvals, waitlists tied to membership state, waivers per participant, and
emergency-contact handling in a club-native way. I am deliberately writing that as a documentation-based
conclusion, not an absolute feature absence claim, because Zeffy’s public materials reviewed here were
strong on pricing and high-level nonprofit positioning, but weaker on these club-native specifics.
Confidence: medium. 6




                                                      15
So the direct strategic answer is:


      • Does Zeffy erode Memba’s fair-pricing advantage? Yes, heavily. A “fairly priced dues/payments/
        events system” is a weak primary story when a real competitor can say “your organization pays
        nothing.” 6
      • What survives? The parts Zeffy does not obviously model: household complexity, volunteer handoff,
        privacy/admin nuance, and activity/waiver workflows for member organizations rather than
        fundraising databases. 77

My bottom-line assessment: Zeffy probably kills “fair pricing” as a standalone wedge. Memba should
treat price as a qualifier—predictable, no lock-in, no weird surcharges—while expecting to win on club-
native domain fit, not on being “the reasonable-priced option.” Confidence: high. 78


Emerging entrants briefing

The newcomers and newly-surfaced competitors that matter most

The most important newly-found direct-convergence name is Communal. Its official positioning is
unusually close to Memba’s intended shape: it is “built for community and member-based organizations”
that run memberships, bookings, programs, and payments together, and its pricing language explicitly
says it follows active members, not seat counts. That is not just adjacent; it directly attacks a future
Memba narrative around fair, active-member-based pricing and a unified operating model for volunteer/
community organizations. The biggest gap in this pass is that I could not verify founding year or funding
from official sources reviewed here, so it remains a high-priority follow-up. Even with that gap, Communal is
the clearest “someone is building toward the same hill” signal I found. 67


Heylo also belongs in the emerging-threat conversation, even though it is no longer brand-new.
TechCrunch says it was founded in 2019 and raised $1.5M in 2022 after bootstrapping to profitability. Since
then, it has clearly converged toward club-like operations: memberships, paid events, waivers, waitlists,
exports, role permissions, and country/language availability broad enough to matter outside the US. If
Memba’s beachhead is outdoor/activity clubs, Heylo is dangerous precisely because it can win clubs that are
culturally “community/event first” before they ever feel the pain that would force them into a true AMS. 79


Givefront is not a direct replacement today, but it is the sort of adjacent startup worth tracking because it
shows active venture attention returning to nonprofit infrastructure. TechCrunch reported a $2M seed and
YC backing in late 2025, with the product aimed at nonprofit financial infrastructure and spend
management. That does not solve membership operations yet, but it is the sort of product that can expand
outward—first into finance ops, later into constituent and member workflows—once it has a trusted base
system. I would watch it for Memba’s broader volunteer-org ambition, not the beachhead. 80


Bono is newer and more distant. TechCrunch reported a $1.6M pre-seed in 2025 for an AI-assisted
charitable giving platform. It is not a Memba competitor today, but it is another signal that donor/nonprofit
workflows are attracting new product energy, especially AI-native product packaging. That matters because
the broader market Memba eventually wants to enter will not be static: new donors, payments, fundraising,
and volunteer tooling will keep appearing around the edges. 76

Communal — because the pricing logic and problem framing converge directly on Memba’s future story.

Canadian and Commonwealth market note
The Canada/Commonwealth correction is real. Amilia is a serious Canadian/North American benchmark in
households, waivers, waitlists, and permissions. SportLoMo has concrete Canadian credibility through
deployments such as Rugby Canada, Volleyball BC, and Softball Ontario. Yapla has a current Canadian
expansion story and free membership positioning. Together, those three change the question in Canada
from “is there anything local?” to “which local/regional alternative does the prospect already know?” 84


In the UK and Ireland, the volunteer-club field is even denser. membermojo is astonishingly cheap and
tailor-made for small clubs. Clubforce is deeply rooted in Irish grassroots sport and volunteer realities.
SportLoMo is strong wherever governing-body registration, compliance, or competition structure matter.
Spond Club is a modern free/low-cost default in grassroots sport. This means Memba cannot assume that
UK/Ireland volunteer clubs are sitting on spreadsheets waiting for a “modern affordable” tool; many already
have modern-enough options.      85




In Australia and New Zealand, TidyHQ is the standout regional benchmark because it combines club-
native breadth, volunteer governance language, strong family memberships, and API/integration
extensibility. It also explicitly markets across Australia, New Zealand, the UK, Ireland, and the US. For
Memba, that makes TidyHQ both a competitor and an existence proof that a modern volunteer-club
platform can travel well across Commonwealth markets. 86


A final regional point on payments: the markets in scope are already full of tools that either price at $0 to
the organization, at very low annual club rates, or with pass-through processing only. Zeffy, HelloAsso,




                                                      17
Spond, Teamup-plus-other-tools, Ticket Tailor, membermojo, SportLoMo club pricing, and Yapla all push
buyer expectations downward in one way or another. That means payment-surcharge sensitivity should be
treated as high across Canada and the Commonwealth—not because every buyer demands $0, but
because a prospect can very plausibly say, “why am I paying a meaningful subscription when I can assemble
something much cheaper?” 87

Differentiation impact

Here is the blunt answer on Memba’s hypotheses.




                                                  21
Fair, predictable pricing by active members is weakened—possibly dead as a primary wedge. Zeffy and
HelloAsso show that “free forever via voluntary contribution” is viable. Spond Club normalizes free club
administration with payment fees. Givebutter offers a free CRM core. membermojo sets a startlingly low
floor for tiny clubs. Ticket Tailor and Teamup make it easy to patch together narrow functions cheaply.
Memba can still be competitively priced, but “we’re fairer than the incumbents” is no longer a strong
opening line on its own. 104


Household/family memberships are still the strongest surviving wedge, but only if Memba goes deeper
than “linked family members.” TidyHQ is strong. Amilia is strong. membermojo is decent but lead-member-
centric. Clubforce and Spond are parent/guardian-friendly, but not obviously full household domain models.
The opportunity is still there if Memba handles one payer, multiple individuals, individual logins/waivers/
profiles, child-to-adult transitions, and nuanced access control better than these products do. 105


Renewal + payment automation is unchanged to moderately weakened. Many tools now do recurring
billing or renewal reminders. The edge survives only if Memba handles the messy cases—calendar-fixed
versus rolling, offline payments, admin corrections, renewal grace logic, partial family changes, late
renewals, and year-to-year volunteer continuity—better than the field. 106


Volunteer-admin simplicity and handoff are strengthened as a differentiation theme. Public materials
across the field talk a lot about features and a lot less about succession, control transfer, reduced
institutional memory loss, and safe volunteer turnover. TidyHQ comes closest by explicitly emphasizing
committee/governance controls, logs, and custom admin access, but even there the market language is still
more “admin permissions” than “board handoff.” This remains a strong Memba narrative if the product
actually operationalizes it. 107


Lightweight activity/event management with waivers and member-only visibility is strengthened,
especially for the beachhead. Plenty of tools do pieces: Spond does waivers and waitlists; SportLoMo does
waivers/consents/compliance; Ticket Tailor does ticketing/check-in; Amilia does waivers and waitlists; Heylo
does events/waitlists/waivers/chat. Very few combine the exact outdoor-club mix of member-state-aware
activity workflows, approvals, emergency context, privacy, volunteer usability, and household logic. That
combination is still open territory. 108


Easy data export and leave-anytime portability are strengthened as trust builders. Several credible
alternatives market exports, CSVs, or Google Sheets connectivity, which means users increasingly expect
portability rather than tolerate lock-in. That makes portability less differentiating as a slogan, but more
differentiating as a proof-of-trust standard—especially if Memba makes full-fidelity household, payment,
waiver, and event export genuinely simple. 109


Open questions and limitations
This pass is strongest on the required gap categories—free models, Commonwealth club-native tools, and
emerging entrants—and intentionally weaker on the original AMS set, which I carried into the revised
ranking without re-auditing every product page in this report. Those carry-forward rankings should be
treated as lower-confidence comparative judgements, not refreshed factual vendor profiles.




                                                      22
I could not verify every requested item symmetrically for every vendor. In particular, public review evidence
was uneven; some products had strong visible public review or testimonial signals, while others had sparse
or hard-to-parse public feedback. I also encountered several cases where an important absence—especially
household nuance or waiver depth—was not explicitly documented either way. Where that happened, I
treated it as “not verified in public sources reviewed,” not as a categorical absence.


The largest follow-up gaps I would most want closed in a future pass are: Communal’s founding/funding/
traction details, a dedicated Canadian audit of Members Village, a fresh side-by-side re-audit of the
original AMS incumbents using 2026 pricing and feature pages, and a more systematic scrape of real
customer complaints for the smaller club-native tools.
