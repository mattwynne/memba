Memba Market and Competitor Research
What the evidence says
The market gap for Memba is real, but it is narrower and more specific than “membership software for any
club.” The strongest evidence points to an underserved segment of volunteer-run outdoor and activity
clubs that need a system for dues, renewals, households/families, private member information,
lightweight communication, and easy admin handoff, but are poorly served by either heavy all-in-one
association systems, creator/community platforms, event tools, or DIY stacks. Existing products usually do
one or two parts well, but not the full combination of volunteer-admin usability + family membership
logic + predictable pricing + outdoor-club fit. 1


The clearest opening is not at the enterprise end. It is in clubs that are too organized for Google Groups,
Mailchimp, Meetup, or Eventbrite, but too price-sensitive or household-heavy for WildApricot or
ClubExpress, and too operationally specific for Memberful or Mighty Networks. Public club websites show a
fragmented current stack: some clubs use WildApricot or ClubExpress for dues, others use Google Groups
and Mailchimp for communications, and public-facing activities often sit on Meetup or Eventbrite. That
fragmentation itself is a strong buying signal if Memba can remove admin work rather than add another
system.   2




The biggest strategic implication is that Memba should not try to out-feature every incumbent on day
one. The evidence supports a wedge around membership operations for volunteer-led outdoor clubs,
with especially strong attention to households, renewals, privacy-aware directories, simple
announcements, exports/Google Sheets sync, and very fast onboarding for the next volunteer who
inherits the job. 3

Across the landscape, the most important pattern is this: the closest “job-to-be-done” competitors are
WildApricot, ClubExpress, memberplanet, and Join It. Everything else is either an adjacent layer, a
creator/community tool, or a DIY stack. Within that top group, WildApricot and ClubExpress are the most
club-native but heavier; Join It is the cleanest usability benchmark; memberplanet is broad but fee-
heavy. That is the competitive lane Memba should enter. 17


Outdoor club market
The public evidence suggests that outdoor/activity clubs are a real but fragmented market. Federations
and directories show meaningful club density: the Federation of Mountain Clubs of BC says it has 54 clubs
with 5,000+ members across BC, Cycling BC maintains a live 2026 list of affiliated clubs/teams/businesses,
and BC Marine Trails maintains regional paddling-club directories. That does not give a precise TAM for
Memba, but it does show a broad and active club ecosystem in the outdoor categories you named. 18


A practical working view of club size from sampled public club pages is: many local volunteer clubs sit
roughly in the tens to low hundreds, while regional legacy clubs can reach several hundred or more.
Examples include Carse Canoe Club at around 40 members, Alki Velo Club at 55+, EVMA at 200+ paid
members, Woking Cycling Club at 200+, Hoosier Hikers Council at 270+, The Rucksack Club at 550+, and
Georgia Appalachian Trail Club at 900+. National or umbrella organizations go much higher, but those are a
different product tier. Based on that sample, Memba’s most plausible initial ICP is something like 30 to 300
members, with occasional wins above that range. 19




                                                     8
Dues levels vary sharply, but a useful split emerges. For clubs without owned facilities, public dues are
often modest: Cleveland Hiking Club lists $30/year, Wanderbirds $20/year, Lambton Outdoor Club $25/
year, Nanaimo Paddlers $40/year, Vancouver Velo Vets $50/year plus separate Cycling BC insurance/
licensing, Mount Baker Bicycle Club $35 individual / $50 family, AMC $50 individual / $75 family, and ADK
$50 adult / $70 family. By contrast, paddling or sailing clubs with equipment, capital contributions, or
boathouse access are much higher: Rideau Canoe Club lists $360 individual / $680 family plus capital
contribution and HST, Washington Canoe Club lists $950 individual / $1,300 family, and UBC Sailing lists
$397 for non-students. In other words, there are really two sub-markets: a large price-sensitive volunteer-
club segment, and a smaller but higher-ARPU facilities/program segment. 20


The market is also clearly volunteer-administered. Hoosier Hikers says it is “run exclusively by volunteers,”
and UBC Sailing describes itself as a “member-run, not-for-profit club.” Club websites and platform docs
also show routine admin tasks such as assigning roles, loading databases, configuring renewal notices, and
exporting rosters. This matters because volunteer turnover changes the buying criteria: the winner is often
not the system with the longest feature list, but the one the next volunteer can understand in one evening.
 21




Current tooling is highly fragmented. Rideau Canoe Club and EVMA visibly run on WildApricot; Red Dragon
Canoe Club and Lambton Outdoor Club use ClubExpress pages; Potomac Mountain Club explicitly says it
uses Google email groups for member communications and Mailchimp for newsletters; public hiking
communities appear on Meetup; and hiking events are common on Eventbrite. That mix strongly suggests
outdoor clubs are not buying an elegant end-to-end platform today—they are assembling one. 22


These clubs do appear willing to pay, but only within tight bounds. They already pay dues, insurance,
governing-body fees, software subscriptions, and payment fees. The caveat is that pricing must feel
proportionate to club revenue and volunteer time saved. Public WildApricot complaints about pricing
cliffs and contact cleanup, plus recurring complaints about event fees and tool fragmentation, show that
willingness to pay exists, but patience for surprise costs does not. 23

A useful way to read that benchmark is through club economics. Lambton Outdoor Club charges $25/year.
A 100-member club at that dues level grosses about $2,500/year before payment fees. On that basis,
WildApricot’s annual 100-contact plan would consume roughly 29% of gross dues, ClubExpress at 100 active
members roughly 20%, and Join It Starter with combined nonprofit and annual discounts roughly 11%, all
before payment processing. That arithmetic helps explain why clubs are price-sensitive even when they
clearly value software. 41


The pricing opportunity for Memba is therefore not “be the absolute cheapest.” It is to be predictable,
explainable, and obviously fair for small clubs. In this market, pricing model clarity may matter almost
as much as list price. The evidence especially argues against opaque contact-count cliffs, stacked platform
fees, and feature gating that hides essential volunteer-admin functions behind expensive tiers. 42


MVP and positioning recommendations

MVP recommendations

The evidence supports an MVP centered on membership operations, but with a few outdoor-club-specific
nuances.




                                                    11
The first priority should be a member and household model, not just a flat list of people. Outdoor clubs
frequently sell family pricing and often need to know which specific household members are joining,
renewing, or attending activities. WildApricot’s long-running family-event pain point is a strong warning
that “family membership” is not solved by just putting multiple names under one payer. Memba should
treat households and individuals as first-class objects, with per-person participation and permissions inside
one billing relationship. 43


The second priority should be a renewal and payments engine that is visibly better than today’s
workarounds: recurring dues, pre-renewal reminders, grace periods, failed-payment handling, offline
payment recording, and easy reactivation of lapsed members. This is the single most common operational
job the membership suites all emphasize, and it is where communications-only or event-led tools fail. 44


The third priority should be volunteer-admin ergonomics: role-based permissions, clean admin handoff,
obvious audit trails, bulk actions, clean CSV import/export, and a first-class Google Sheets export/sync
story. Public complaints about manual cleanup, broad admin complexity, and multi-tool workflows all point
here. For this market, “the next volunteer can take over without a training project” is a product feature. 45


The fourth priority should be private member value without trying to become a social network: a privacy-
aware member directory, private pages/files, simple announcements, and member-only content. That
keeps Memba competitive with the baseline expectations set by WildApricot, ClubExpress, memberplanet,
and Join It without drifting into the complexity of Mighty or Discourse. 46


The fifth priority is to move event/trip management from “later” to “lightweight soon after launch.” The
evidence does not say Memba must launch with full expedition logistics, waivers, leader management, and
route planning. It does say that clubs live through calendars and activities, and that a membership system
with no member-only RSVP, waitlist, or activity linkage will feel incomplete quickly. A sensible sequence is:
launch with membership ops + directory + announcements, then add member-only events/trips, RSVP/
waitlist, and activity-specific attendee lists before attempting a richer trip product. 47


What to defer is equally important: full site builder competition with WordPress/WildApricot, broad
community feeds like Mighty, deep forum workflows like Discourse, advanced email marketing, and public
event discovery. Those are all crowded areas with strong incumbents, and they are not the clearest reason a
volunteer hiking or paddling club changes tools. 48


Positioning recommendations

The strongest positioning is not “another membership platform.” It is:


Memba is the membership platform for volunteer-run outdoor clubs that need simple dues,
households, and member operations without the bloat of an association suite or the hacks of a DIY
stack. 49


That can be sharpened into four practical positioning messages.


The first is “built for volunteer admins.” This directly contrasts with systems whose breadth implies
training overhead and with DIY stacks that assume technical ownership. It should be demonstrated with
onboarding, migration, templates, and handoff experiences, not just copy. 50



                                                     12
The second is “households done properly.” This is one of the clearest under-served problems in the
evidence. WildApricot bundles, Join It group/couples memberships, and Memberful group subscriptions all
address adjacent needs, but none are clearly optimized for outdoor-club household + activity participation
flows. 51


The third is “predictable pricing for small clubs.” Price sensitivity is strong, but it is especially sensitive to
pricing models that feel unfair: contact cliffs, layered platform fees, or per-ticket event taxes. Memba should
consider pricing around active members or households, not all contacts, with household logic included
rather than treated as a premium add-on. 52


The fourth is “works with the spreadsheet reality clubs already have.” Google Sheets export/sync is not
a minor convenience here; it is a trust bridge for volunteer-run groups that already live in Sheets, email, and
committee handoffs. A credible migration story from CSV, WildApricot exports, and ClubExpress/member
rosters will materially reduce sales friction. 53


Risks, experiments, and limitations
The biggest product risk is that membership admin alone may not be enough to trigger switching.
Clubs may say they want better renewals, but still make decisions primarily around event/trip workflows
because that is where member value is most visible. The right experiment is not a generic interview round;
it is a message test comparing “households + renewals” against “dues + trips in one place,” followed by a
clickable prototype that includes both join/renew and activity RSVP flows. 54


The next risk is price compression. Small clubs do pay, but they compare software cost to modest dues
revenue and often already tolerate ugly workarounds. The best validation experiment is to recruit pilot
clubs in the 30–300 member range and ask for a real paid pilot, not just a design partnership. If clubs
love the concept but resist even low-friction annual pricing after seeing the product, that is a signal about
market depth. 55


A third risk is migration friction. Many clubs already have rosters, lapsed members, and mailing habits
embedded in old systems. Validation here should be operational: build import templates for at least CSV,
WildApricot exports, and ClubExpress exports, then measure time-to-live with a real club. If migration
takes days instead of hours, volunteer buyers may not switch even if the product is better. 56


A fourth risk is family/household edge cases. This area is strategically promising precisely because it is
messy. The solution is to recruit several clubs that already publish family dues or family membership rules
and use them as design partners for the data model before locking the MVP. 57


There are also a few limitations in the research. I found strong public evidence on competitor pricing,
positioning, and recurring pain points, but less uniform public evidence on family support depth for
every vendor, and no single high-quality public dataset that precisely sizes the North American “volunteer-
run outdoor club” software market. The market-overview conclusions therefore rely on a combination of
official vendor sources and a sample of public club websites across hiking, mountaineering, cycling,
paddling, and related outdoor clubs. Pricing for Mailchimp and some event tools is also localized on official
pages, so public list pricing can vary by region or promotional state. 58

The highest-confidence conclusion remains the same: Memba has a credible wedge if it goes narrow. The
most defensible starting point is membership operations for volunteer-run outdoor clubs, with standout
execution on households, renewals, admin simplicity, privacy-aware directories, exports/Sheets, and
lightweight member communication, then a near-follow-on into member-only trip/event workflows.
That is where the evidence most clearly shows incumbent friction and willingness to switch. 59
